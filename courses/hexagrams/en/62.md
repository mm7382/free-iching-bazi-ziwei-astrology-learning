*Overdo the small things a little; do not cross the line on the large ones.*

64 Hexagrams / 62

Overdo the small things a little; do not cross the line on the large ones.

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Xiao Guo: Success. Steadfast correctness is beneficial. Small matters are possible; great matters are not. The flying bird leaves its call. Going upward is unsuitable; going downward is suitable. Great good fortune.

The Tuan commentary reads Xiao Guo as the small exceeding and thereby succeeding, provided it keeps correct and moves with the time: the yielding lines hold the centre, so small matters go well, while the firm lines are out of place, so great matters cannot be undertaken. The flying bird leaves its cry behind, and going down accords while going up runs against. The Xiang image is thunder above the mountain, with the noble person erring slightly towards respect, towards grief in mourning, and towards thrift in spending.

Zhen above, Gen below. Thunder sounds on the mountain top and the sound does not carry far, so this is the hexagram of going a little past. Xiao Guo is about scale: in harmless small matters such as respect, mourning and thrift, it is better to do slightly more, but in great matters do not cross the line at all. The judgement uses the bird leaving its cry to say that downward is right and upward is not, and that a lower posture is what brings fortune. The lines run from a bird flying high too early, through reaching the grandmother rather than the grandfather, refusing to guard a little more and being harmed, keeping within bounds, dense clouds that do not rain, and finally overshooting altogether. This is a season for gathering in: make the task small and the posture low.

Although the judgment gives success, beneficial steadfastness, and great good fortune, that fortune explicitly depends on staying low rather than rising. The commentary directly rules out great undertakings. Three lines bring misfortune: the first's flying bird, the third, and the top's calamity. Only the second and fourth offer no blame. Outcomes depend entirely on maintaining small proportions: lowering brings passage, raising brings misfortune. Both possibilities coexist with a sharp contrast, hence the rating: Neutral. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: attend carefully to small matters, keep a low posture, and control the size of each move. Avoid: leaping over levels on a big undertaking, or ignoring a warning because the small things are going well.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Zhen above, Gen below: stopping underneath, movement above — only a small exceeding.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Xiao Guo.

- State how Xiao Guo differs from Da Guo.

- Point out that Small Exceeding and Nourishing use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Xiao Guo is Gen: two broken lines with a solid third. The upper is Zhen: a solid fourth line with two broken above. Only the third and fourth lines are solid and the other four are broken, so the solid lines sit exactly at the centre.

In Da Guo the four central lines are solid and the weight presses on the middle; in Xiao Guo only two central lines are solid and the weight is far lighter. So although both speak of exceeding, Xiao Guo exceeds only slightly. It reads as suited to small matters: the structure cannot carry a large move, and forcing one would not be exceeding a little but collapsing altogether.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a thinly staffed team can take on one more small job but not a large one. Xiao Guo describes that structure.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Six at the beginning: A flying bird brings misfortune.

The Xiang commentary says of the flying bird that nothing can be done about it, a phrase close to a sigh. Rising at the very start goes against the instruction to stay low.

Right at the beginning you fly high like the bird, and misfortune follows. You went up when the time asked you to go down, and by then there is no repairing it.

Competing for visibility and position before you have found your footing carries the most risk. The opening will wait for you; the fall may not find anyone there to help you up.

Are your feet steady under the step you are rushing to take?

### Six in the second place: Passing the grandfather, meeting the grandmother; not reaching the ruler, meeting the minister. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says that not reaching the ruler is right, because a minister should not go past the ruler. Stopping here is exactly why no fault attaches.

You pass the grandfather and meet the grandmother; you aim for the ruler and reach only the minister. Stopping there brings no fault, because you were never meant to go past.

Asking to see the decision-maker and getting the contact point instead is usually not a failure. Making your case clearly through the right level is both safer and more effective than forcing your way up.

Is the level you want to skip really one you need to skip?

### Nine in the third place: Failing to take exceptional precautions, someone following may harm you. Misfortune.

The Xiang commentary exclaims at how severe this misfortune is. Refusing to exceed in the matter of guarding means those who follow may do the injuring.

You will not take the extra precaution at the point where extra precaution belongs, and the people close by turn out to be the ones who harm you. In this season, guarding is precisely the small thing you are allowed to overdo.

The moment you decide everyone around you is on your side and no safeguards are needed is usually the most exposed. Putting contracts, access and responsibilities in writing is not distrust; it gives the relationship somewhere to retreat to.

What did you skip confirming because you assumed nothing would go wrong?

### Nine in the fourth place: No blame. Do not exceed; meet what comes. Advancing brings danger: be watchful. Do not persist forever.

The Xiang commentary notes that the position is not fitting, so meeting without exceeding cannot be forced, and warns that advancing brings danger and that no single approach should be held to forever, since this state of affairs will not last.

No fault attaches, because instead of forcing your way past you meet things as they come. Going forward carries danger and calls for caution, and no single method should be kept in place indefinitely.

Do not push and do not force; what is meant to meet you will. Remember too that the present workaround suits only the present, and should change when the conditions do rather than hardening into permanent policy.

Which temporary workaround have you started treating as a fixed rule?

### Six in the fifth place: Dense clouds, no rain, from our western outskirts. The duke shoots with a corded arrow, taking what is in the cave.

The Xiang commentary says the clouds bring no rain because they have already risen too high. The prince takes with a corded arrow whatever is hiding in the burrow, which is a modest catch rather than a great one.

The clouds bank up thickly and no rain falls, drifting in from the western outskirts. The prince can only draw out what is hiding in the burrow: there is a result, but a small one.

When every condition looks present and nothing comes of it, you have usually set yourself too high to touch the ground. Take the small gain and stop holding out for the downpour.

How many small gains has waiting for the big one already cost you?

### Six at the top: Not meeting, but overshooting. The flying bird is caught. Misfortune: this is calamity and error.

The Xiang commentary says that not meeting but exceeding shows one has already gone too high. Calamity and self-inflicted trouble together mean the harm comes from both outside and inside.

What should have met you does not, and you sail straight past it; the bird flies far off and misfortune follows, compounded from outside and from your own hand. The end of small exceeding is exceeding too far.

Pressing on after you have already gone past the mark rarely costs you only one thing. Stopping, admitting the error and bringing people back matters far more than adding another push.

Is there something you have known for a while you have overshot?

## Quick Quiz

- The third and the fourth

- The first and the sixth

- The second and the fifth

**The third and fourth lines (Correct)** : Correct. The two solid lines occupy the two central positions.

**The first and the sixth** : Solid lines at both ends form Yi · Nourishment.

**The second and the fifth** : Those two lines are broken.

- Da Guo has four solid central lines; Xiao Guo only two

- They are the same

- Xiao Guo has no solid lines

**Da Guo has four solid middle lines; Xiao Guo has only two (Correct)** : Correct. Their amounts differ greatly, as do the degrees of exceeding.

**They are the same** : The number of solid lines differs.

**Xiao Guo has no solid lines** : Xiao Guo's third and fourth lines are solid.

- The structure carries only a small exceeding

- Nothing can be done

- The bigger the better

**The structure supports only a small degree of exceeding (Correct)** : Correct. Forcing a large undertaking exceeds much more than a little.

**Nothing can be done** : Small matters are possible; the hexagram does not forbid everything.

**The bigger the better** : That is opposite to this structure.
