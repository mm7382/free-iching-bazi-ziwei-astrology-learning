64 Hexagrams / 28

The load is beyond the structure; lighten it before it gives.

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Da Guo: The ridgepole bends. It is beneficial to proceed. Success.

The Tuan commentary reads the sagging ridgepole as weakness at both root and tip, with firmness in excess yet centred; gentle and pleased movement makes advance worthwhile. The Xiang image is the lake submerging the trees, with the noble person standing alone without fear and withdrawing from the world without distress.

Dui above, Xun below. Four solid lines mass in the middle with a single yielding line at each end, which the commentary describes as weak at both root and tip. Neither end can hold, so the ridgepole bends. This is a load beyond what the structure can carry. The judgement still favours going somewhere, because standing still is not a way out; you have to work your way through. The posture offered is to stand alone unafraid and stay settled even when no one understands. The lines show both a ridgepole holding up well and a crossing that goes over your head.

Weak ends and a bending roof beam describe a real danger of excessive load. The third nine's bending beam and the top six's drowning both explicitly bring misfortune, hence the rating: Rather Unfavorable. Yet the judgment's beneficial proceeding and success, along with the fourth nine's raised beam and good fortune, preserve room to find a way through, so it is not the lowest rating. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: name the load that is too heavy and put your resources on the one joint that matters most. Avoid: keeping up appearances while the reinforcement waits, or trying to carry every problem at once.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Dui above, Xun below: four solid lines in the middle, broken at both ends.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Da Guo.

- Explain why heavy in the middle and weak at the ends reads as exceeding.

- Work out that Great Exceeding with its halves swapped is Inner Truth.

The lower trigram of Da Guo is Xun: a broken first line with two solid above. The upper is Dui: two solid lines with a broken sixth. Lines two through five are solid; only the first and last are broken.

The weight is all in the middle while the supporting ends are yielding. That proportion reads as exceeding: not wrongdoing, but going beyond what the structure can bear. It is the inverse of Yi — Yi is hollow in the middle, Da Guo is full there. Note that “exceeding” describes imbalance, not a moral judgement.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a project puts almost all its resources into the middle build phase, leaving one person each for discovery and maintenance. Da Guo describes that structure of thin ends.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Six at the beginning: Spread white thatch beneath. No blame.

White rushes are laid down as a mat before anything is set upon them. The Xiang commentary reads this as the yielding taking the lower place: care upon care, and so no fault.

Spreading clean rushes underneath before placing the vessel is extreme care. At the opening of a heavily loaded situation, that carefulness is what keeps the line free of fault.

At the start of a heavy commitment, make the groundwork and the safeguards a little more thorough than strictly needed. The care that looks excessive now pays out later.

Before this heavy task begins, what is one more layer of protection you could add?

### Nine in the second place: A withered poplar sprouts new shoots; an older man gains a young wife. Nothing unfavorable.

A withered poplar puts out new shoots; an older man takes a young wife. The Xiang commentary calls this an association that exceeds the norm, yet firm and yielding complement each other, so nothing is unfavourable.

A dry tree sends out fresh growth. A pairing that looks out of line brings life instead, which is why nothing counts against it. The line is about unusual matches in unusual times.

Extraordinary periods allow unlikely combinations, such as pairing an experienced hand with a newcomer so each covers what the other lacks.

Which unlikely pairing would actually cover the gap you have?

### Nine in the third place: The ridgepole bends. Misfortune.

The ridgepole sags and this goes badly. The Xiang commentary points out that no support can be accepted at this stage, which is the real cause of the trouble.

The beam bends and the structure is heading for failure. The commentary locates the problem in an unwillingness to hear offers of help; standing alone on sheer hardness leaves the danger unresolved.

When the load is clearly beyond you, refusing help is the most dangerous move available. Admitting you cannot hold it and asking is what creates room to adjust.

Is there someone you have not yet asked for help with the thing you are holding up?

### Nine in the fourth place: The ridgepole rises. Good fortune. Other entanglements bring regret.

The ridgepole rises rather than sagging, hence good fortune; the Xiang commentary says it does not bend towards what is below. If other attachments pull at it, there is cause for regret.

The beam arches and holds, so the line reads well. But side interests and entanglements still bring regret. Concentration is the condition attached to the good outcome.

Once you have finally got the situation holding, keep your resources on the main line. Starting something new now tends to loosen the structure you just steadied.

What side project is taking the strength you need for the main beam?

### Nine in the fifth place: A withered poplar flowers; an older woman gains a young husband. No blame and no praise.

The withered poplar puts out flowers; the Xiang commentary asks how long that can last, and finds the pairing of an older woman with a young man unbecoming. There is neither fault nor praise.

Flowers on a dead tree are pleasant to look at and cannot last. The arrangement is not wrong, but there is nothing in it worth commending. Visible flourishing without a base under it does not carry on.

A brief good result with no underlying conditions behind it is fine to accept, but not to expand on. Check first whether it can happen again.

Could your recent good result repeat itself in a different month?

### Six at the top: Crossing too far, water covers the head. Misfortune, but no blame.

Wading too deep, the water closes over the head. The Xiang commentary says the misfortune cannot be blamed, since the act came from necessity or from duty.

The crossing is too deep and the water goes over you, which is a bad outcome. Because it was unavoidable, no blame attaches. The misfortune is real; the absence of blame is a recognition of the intent behind it.

Some efforts exceed what you can carry even when the motive is sound, so sound the depth before you step in. If you are already under, getting out comes before working out who was right.

Could you live with the worst case of the thing you are about to commit to?

## Quick Quiz

- The first and the sixth

- The second and the fifth

- The third and the fourth

**The first and sixth lines (Correct)** : Correct. The four middle lines are all solid.

**The second and the fifth** : Those two lines are solid, each at the center of a trigram.

**The third and the fourth** : Those two lines are also solid.

- One is full in the middle and the other hollow; every line is reversed

- Their halves are swapped

- They differ by one line

**One has a solid middle and the other an empty middle; every line is opposite (Correct)** : Correct. Yi is solid at the ends and empty within; Da Guo is solid within and empty at the ends.

**Their halves are swapped** : Exchanging Yi's trigrams produces Xiao Guo, not Da Guo.

**They differ by one line** : All six corresponding lines are opposite.

- Imbalance: going beyond what the structure can bear

- Doing something wrong

- Something is over

**An imbalance in proportions exceeds the structure's carrying capacity (Correct)** : Correct. It is not a moral judgment.

**Doing something wrong** : The hexagram concerns structural proportions, not right and wrong.

**Something is over** : Here “exceeding” means going beyond, not the past.
