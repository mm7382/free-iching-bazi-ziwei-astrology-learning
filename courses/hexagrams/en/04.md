# Meng · Youthful Inexperience: Original Text, Plain-Language Explanation, and Six Lines

Written by [Wei Chun Chuang](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/about.html)

*Ask honestly; openness is what teaches you*

64 Hexagrams / 04

Ask honestly; openness is what teaches you

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Meng: Success. I do not seek the inexperienced youth; the youth seeks me. At the first divination, I answer. Repeating it twice or three times is disrespectful; if disrespectful, I do not answer. Steadfast correctness is beneficial.

The Tuan commentary places danger below the mountain: facing it, one halts, and that is Meng. Nurturing what is correct in the unformed is described as sacred work. The Xiang commentary sees a spring emerging at the mountain's foot.

Meng is the state of not yet knowing, and the asking that follows. A spring hides beneath the mountain, waiting to be found. The judgement is blunt about how learning works: the student comes to the teacher, not the reverse. Ask sincerely once and you get an answer; ask carelessly again and again and nothing useful comes back. Approach this with an open, modest mind.

Meng offers success but requires sincere seeking of instruction. A lack of initiative or a careless attitude brings no benefit. Overall this is a neutral stage of early learning, neither fortunate nor unfortunate. Benefit lies in steadfastness and sincerity, hence the rating: Balanced.

Worth doing: admit what you do not know, ask a specific question, and follow a method you trust. Avoid: guessing cleverly without checking, or asking repeatedly while practising nothing.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Gen above, Kan below: danger underneath, stopping on top — nothing yet seen clearly.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Meng.

- Explain why danger below and stopping above reads as not yet clear.

- Work out that Youthful Ignorance with its halves swapped is Obstruction.

The lower trigram of Meng is Kan: one yang in the middle with yin on either side. The upper is Gen: one yang at the very top with two yin beneath. Only the second and sixth lines of the figure are solid. Read upward, the pit of Kan comes first and is then stopped by Gen.

Kan is a pit, a lack of clear sight; Gen is stopping, a blockage. Meng puts the unclear below and the blockage above, picturing someone inside a situation with no line of sight out of it. Tradition uses this hexagram to talk about teaching, and the structure is why: someone has to open the wall above before the person inside can see.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a newcomer is dropped into an undocumented system and cannot even form a question, because they do not yet know what to ask. Meng describes that structure; it does not say the person cannot learn.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Six at the beginning: Opening the inexperienced. It is beneficial to apply discipline and remove shackles. Continuing in this way brings regret.

The Xiang commentary says that applying discipline here serves to establish the rule. Shackles have to come off before anyone can walk.

The first step in learning is setting the rules and removing what restricts you. Rushing ahead before clearing the obstacles only adds difficulty.

Before starting something new, put the necessary structure in place and drop the old habits that get in the way.

Which old habit or mistaken assumption do you need to clear out before you start?

### Nine in the second place: Embracing the inexperienced brings good fortune; taking a wife brings good fortune. The son can manage the household.

The Xiang commentary says the son manages the household because firm and yielding meet. Taking in learners of every level is what makes a house prosper.

Making room for learners at every level brings good results. Whoever shoulders the responsibility lifts the whole.

If you teach or lead, be patient with people at different levels. If you are learning, accept the guidance. Either way it works out.

Are you tolerant enough here, and are you willing to accept guidance or carry the responsibility?

### Six in the third place: Do not take this woman as a wife. Seeing a man of wealth, she loses possession of herself. Nothing is beneficial.

The Xiang commentary says not to take this bride, because the conduct is not in keeping. Drawn by wealth alone, one loses oneself.

Do not chase an arrangement built purely on material gain or surface shine. You lose your own footing and end up with nothing.

If an opportunity attracts you only on the surface but asks you to give up your principles or yourself, it is not a good one.

Has a surface attraction pulled your attention away from what actually matters here?

### Six in the fourth place: Confined in inexperience. Regret.

The Xiang commentary says the regret of trapped ignorance comes from standing alone, far from anything real.

Staying shut inside what you do not know, refusing to grow or ask, leads only to regret and loss.

If you refuse to learn anything new or deny your own gaps, the present state keeps costing you. The attitude has to change first.

What have you been refusing to admit you do not understand, and why?

### Six in the fifth place: Childlike inexperience. Good fortune.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune of the childlike learner to being compliant and receptive.

Learning with a child's openness, without assuming you already understand, is the most favourable state this hexagram describes.

Look at the problem as a beginner would. Drop your assumptions, ask plainly, and something useful comes back.

Are you modest enough here to look at this again as a beginner would?

### Nine at the top: Striking at inexperience. It is unfavorable to act as a raider, beneficial to defend against raiders.

The Xiang commentary says that using force to repel intruders keeps those above and below in accord. Correction is for defence, not for attack.

Force applied to ignorance is useful for defending and guiding, harmful when used to invade. Influence should direct, not control.

If you have influence, use it to clear obstacles for others and point them somewhere good, not to hold them down.

Is your influence over other people helping them grow, or quietly limiting them?

## Quick Quiz

- The second and the sixth

- The first and the fourth

- The third and the fifth

**The second and sixth lines (Correct)** : Correct. Lower Kan's solid line is second; upper Gen's solid line is sixth.

**The first and the fourth** : That would be Zhen below and above. Meng's first line is broken.

**The third and the fifth** : That is another arrangement. Meng's third and fifth lines are both broken.

- It blocks the top, so there is no view out

- It creates the pit below

- It sets the figure in motion

**It blocks the top, preventing a view outward (Correct)** : Correct. Gen means stopping; above, it covers the exit.

**It creates the pit below** : The lower Kan trigram creates the pit. Gen is above.

**It sets the figure in motion** : Movement belongs to Zhen, which is absent from Meng.

- Jiǎn · Obstruction

- Zhūn · Difficult Beginning

- Bo · Splitting Apart

**Jian · Obstruction (Correct)** : Correct. Gen below and Kan above form Hexagram 39, Jian.

**Zhūn · Difficult Beginning** : Zhun has Zhen below and Kan above; its lower trigram is not Gen.

**Bo · Splitting Apart** : Bo has Kun below and Gen above, with three broken lower lines.
