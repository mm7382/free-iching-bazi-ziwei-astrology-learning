# Dun · Retreat: Original Text, Plain-Language Explanation, and Six Lines

*Step back in good time, cleanly and with your dignity intact.*

64 Hexagrams / 33

Step back in good time, cleanly and with your dignity intact.

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Dun: Success. Small steadfastness is beneficial.

The Tuan commentary says success here comes from the retreat itself: withdrawing at the right moment is the success. The yielding line is steadily growing, so only small matters can be held correct. The Xiang image is the mountain under heaven, with the noble person keeping distance from small-minded people without harsh words, holding dignity instead.

Gen below, Qian above: a mountain standing beneath heaven, the image of knowing when to step back. Retreat here is not flight. It is reading the situation and deliberately opening distance, which is why the judgement calls it successful. Because the yielding force is still rising, only a limited range can be held, so the text grants correctness in small things. The lines run from the danger of leaving last, through a resolve bound as tight as oxhide, to retreats held back by attachment, by fondness, then a graceful one, and finally an unhurried withdrawal with room to spare. The point throughout: leaving early and cleanly is safer than staying on to endure.

The judgment explicitly gives success, the fifth nine a commendable retreat with steadfast good fortune, and the top nine an unburdened retreat with nothing unfavorable. Conditions improve toward the end, showing timely withdrawal as an effective exit. Although the first six warns of danger in late retreat and the third nine of distress from entanglement, the overall direction is positive, hence the rating: Good. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: recognise a losing position early, withdraw clearly, and keep your bearing while you do it. Avoid: fighting on out of attachment, trading provocations, or leaving and then drifting back into the same conflict.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Qian above, Gen below: a whole Qian above a stopping mountain — a structure of retreat.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Dun.

- Explain why retreat is not the same as losing.

- Say that swapping the halves turns Retreat into Great Accumulating.

The lower trigram of Dun is Gen: two broken lines with a solid third. The upper is Qian, all solid. Lines three through six are solid; the bottom two are broken.

Two yin lines have grown from below while a whole Qian still stands above. Structurally this reads as retreat: not defeat, but recognising that the conditions underneath have changed and giving up the position. The point here is timing and direction — retreat is an action, and it must end somewhere that holds; it is not a rout.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a lead notices the supporting resources have moved elsewhere and deliberately pulls the project back to its core. Dun describes that structure of deliberate withdrawal; it does not say the project failed.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Six at the beginning: At the tail of retreat. Danger. Do not proceed.

The Xiang commentary says the danger of being at the tail of the retreat brings no disaster as long as you go no further. The position is poor, but stillness keeps it from becoming harm.

You have dragged out the leaving until you are last, which is the worst place to stand. Pushing forward now only deepens the danger. Holding still and making no new move is what protects you.

When you notice you are the last one still in a situation that has gone wrong, stop every new commitment. Do not throw more time or money at it hoping to recover the position.

What have you known for a while you should pull out of, and kept putting off?

### Six in the second place: Held with yellow oxhide, which none can loosen.

The Xiang commentary reads the yellow oxhide binding as firmness of will. Bound this tightly, nothing can work it loose.

The resolve is tied as fast as a strap of yellow oxhide. This line is not about being trapped. It is about fixing your own position deliberately, so no amount of persuasion shifts it.

Once you have decided to leave or to stay out of something, write the reasons down for yourself. When people keep pressing, answer from those reasons rather than reopening the argument each time.

Would your decision survive being talked at for a third time?

### Nine in the third place: Entangled retreat, with illness and danger. Keeping servants brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary calls a retreat held back by ties a state of illness and exhaustion, fit only for small domestic affairs and not for great undertakings. The master-and-servant wording belongs to its ancient setting and does not reflect present-day values.

You want to withdraw and something keeps pulling you back, which leaves you tired and exposed. In this state you can only handle the small things near at hand. Taking on anything large will cost you more than it returns.

When obligation or loyalty stops you leaving cleanly, shrink your workload to what you can actually carry. Turn down new large commitments for now and revisit them once you have recovered.

What exactly is holding you in place when you already want to go?

### Nine in the fourth place: Willing retreat brings good fortune for the noble person, but not for the petty person.

The Xiang commentary contrasts two responses to the same attachment: the noble person can retreat from what they love, and the small-minded person cannot, which is where the difficulty lies.

The hard part of this line is stepping back from something you genuinely like. Those with room in them can let go and move on. Those who cannot are held in place by their own fondness.

The hardest thing to release is usually the project or the relationship you actually love. People who let go for the sake of the longer run tend to find their next place sooner than those who hold on.

The thing you least want to give up, does it still suit who you are now?

### Nine in the fifth place: Commendable retreat. Steadfastness brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune of an admirable retreat to a will that is properly set. The motive is straight and the timing is right.

This is the best kind of withdrawal in the whole hexagram: well timed and well executed. Because the motive is sound and the decision is clear, holding correct goes well, with nothing left to sour behind you.

Finish the handover properly, say what needs saying, and thank the people who deserve it. Leaving that way is remembered as professional rather than as walking out.

If you left now, how would you want people to describe the way you left?

### Nine at the top: Unburdened retreat. Nothing unfavorable.

The Xiang commentary says an unhurried retreat carries no disadvantage because there is nothing left in doubt. The base text keeps the older character for the negative here.

You have withdrawn to the furthest and most spacious position, with no hesitation left in you, so nothing works against you. Real ease comes from no longer arguing with yourself about whether you should have gone.

When you can talk about that chapter some time later without bitterness, you have genuinely left it. That is also when there is room for something new.

What is still unresolved in you that makes you keep glancing back?

**The whole Qian trigram remains above; retreat recognizes that conditions below have changed (Correct)** : Correct. Retreat is an action: withdraw to a position that can support you.

**Because the figure has no yin lines** : Dun's first and second lines are broken.

**Because the name means winning** : Dun means retreat, not victory.
