## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Ji Ji: Success in small matters. Steadfast correctness is beneficial. Good fortune at first, disorder in the end.

The Tuan commentary says the success of Ji Ji is the success of small things, and that correctness is favoured because firm and yielding are each properly placed, this being the one hexagram in which every line sits in its correct position. Early good fortune comes from the yielding line holding the centre; the disorder at the end comes from stopping once the crossing is made, when the way runs out. The Xiang image is water over fire, with the noble person anticipating trouble and guarding against it in advance.

Kan above, Li below. Water sits over fire, the pot is on and the heat is right: the crossing has been made. Ji Ji is the only hexagram where all six lines occupy their correct places, and it looks complete, yet the judgement grants only small success and says plainly that it begins well and ends in disorder. Completion is itself the dangerous moment, because the order starts to loosen as soon as you decide you have arrived. The lines run from dragging the wheel and wetting the tail, through a loss that returns of itself in seven days, a three-year campaign won at the cost of exhaustion, rags kept ready against a leak, a small offering that suits the season better than a great one, and finally a crossing that soaks the head. The time to start guarding is the moment it is done.

The judgment gives both ends: small success and beneficial steadfastness are positive, yet initial fortune ends in disorder. Its commentary explicitly locates danger in stopping, when the way becomes exhausted. No line says misfortune, but none offers supreme fortune either: the first nine's no blame, second six's recovery in seven days, and fifth nine's real blessing are positive. The third's three-year conquest is exhausting, the fourth stays vigilant all day, and the top wets its head in danger. The most correct arrangement has the least momentum; outcomes depend on continuing after completion, hence the rating: Neutral. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: organise handover and maintenance as soon as something is complete, and keep checking for small changes. Avoid: treating success as the end point, relaxing your standards, or overlooking small errors as they accumulate.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Kan above, Li below: the six lines alternate perfectly, each in its proper place.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Ji Ji.

- Explain why perfect order carries a caution.

- Say that swapping the halves turns After Completion into Before Completion.

The lower trigram of Ji Ji is Li: solid, broken, solid. The upper is Kan: broken, solid, broken. From the bottom up the figure runs solid, broken, solid, broken, solid, broken — a perfect alternation, with yang in every odd place and yin in every even one.

Only two of the sixty-four hexagrams alternate perfectly, and Ji Ji is one of them. Every line sits where tradition holds it ought to, hence the name: already across. The caution comes from exactly that — once complete, there is no room to advance, and the next step can only be a loosening. This hexagram is about maintenance, not celebration.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a system has just met every target, and the work ahead is maintenance rather than another sprint. Ji Ji describes that structure.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Nine at the beginning: Dragging the wheels, wetting the tail. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says that dragging the wheel is without fault in principle, meaning the slowing is a deliberate restraint rather than a mistake.

You hold the wheel back so the cart does not run, and the tail gets wet in the crossing, with no fault attaching. Slowing down right after getting across is a choice you made.

Just after reaching a goal is when it is easiest to press the advantage further. Tapping the brake yourself, and allowing the moment to look less impressive, is what protects what you gained.

After finishing something, do you usually rest first or start the next thing immediately?

### Six in the second place: The woman loses her carriage screen. Do not pursue; regain it in seven days.

The Xiang commentary says the recovery in seven days comes from holding the middle way. The lost item is the screen on a carriage.

A woman loses the screen from her carriage, and there is no need to chase it: in seven days it comes back. Keep the proper rhythm, neither panicking nor pursuing, and what was lost returns in the ordinary course.

When something is lost, an opening passes, or someone misreads you, chasing it often pushes it further away. Give it time and carry on as usual, and most things settle back where they belong.

What would happen to the thing you are chasing if you simply left it alone?

### Nine in the third place: Gaozong campaigns against the Gui Fang, conquering them in three years. Do not employ petty people.

Gaozong is King Wu Ding of Shang, and the Guifang were a northwestern people. The Xiang commentary gives one word, exhaustion, to a victory that took three years, and the text warns against employing small-minded people afterwards.

Gaozong campaigns against the Guifang and takes three years to prevail, and the commentary offers only one word: worn out. After the war, be careful whom you appoint, and keep out those who serve only themselves.

Some fights cost you so much that winning leaves you depleted. What matters more is who holds the ground afterwards; the wrong successor can lose three years of work in a season.

Whose hands are the results you worked so hard for in now?

### Six in the fourth place: Fine clothing turns to rags. Be watchful all day.

The Xiang commentary says the day-long vigilance rests on genuine doubt: the wariness has a basis. Fine silk and worn rags are carried on a boat to plug leaks.

Rags are kept aboard, ready to stop a seam from leaking, and the watch is kept all day. The surface looks calm and the crack is already there beneath it.

A system still running does not mean nothing is wrong. Get the backups, the spare capacity and the fallback plan ready, because that unease you cannot articulate usually has a cause.

What is the thing that faintly worries you and that you have not put into words?

### Nine in the fifth place: The eastern neighbor's slaughtered ox does not equal the western neighbor's simple spring offering, which truly receives blessing.

The Xiang commentary says the eastern neighbour's ox does not match the western neighbour's timing, and that receiving the blessing in substance is great good fortune arriving. The western offering is a plain and modest one.

The eastern neighbour slaughters an ox for a grand rite and gains less than the western neighbour's simple offering, because the latter truly receives the blessing. The difference is not scale but whether it suits the season.

The same intention needs only a sentence when the timing is right, and cannot land however elaborate it is when the timing is wrong. Before you spend the effort, ask whether this is the moment for it.

Is now actually the right time for the thing you are about to do at full scale?

### Six at the top: Wetting the head. Danger.

The Xiang commentary asks how such a state can last. At the first line only the tail was wet; at the top the head goes under, the crossing having gone from shallow to deep.

You cross until the water covers your head, and it is dangerous. What began as a wet tail ends with you fully in the water, and that cannot be held for long.

When you find you are wholly submerged in one thing with no room to breathe, that is not commitment but the signal before you go under. Find the bank now and stop going forward.

What in your life has already risen above your neck?

## Quick Quiz

- The six lines alternate perfectly: yang in odd places, yin in even

- All six lines are yang

- The halves are identical

**All six lines alternate: odd positions yang, even positions yin (Correct)** : Correct. Only two of the 64 hexagrams have fully alternating arrangements.

**All six lines are yang** : Six yang lines form Qian.

**The halves are identical** : Ji Ji's upper and lower trigrams are Kan and Li, which differ.

- Once complete there is no room to advance; the next step can only loosen

- Because order is bad

- Because Kan always means danger

**Completion leaves no room to advance; the next step can only loosen the arrangement (Correct)** : Correct. This hexagram therefore concerns maintenance rather than celebration.

**Because order is bad** : The hexagram does not say that; it says the positions are already fully occupied.

**Because Kan always means danger** : The reminder comes from the full arrangement rather than one trigram alone.

- Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle)

- Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle)

- Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above)

**Kan · Water (broken top and bottom, solid middle) (Correct)** : Correct. Broken fourth, solid fifth, broken sixth.

**Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle)** : Li is Ji Ji's lower trigram.

**Xun · Wind (one broken line below, two solid above)** : Ji Ji's sixth line is broken.
