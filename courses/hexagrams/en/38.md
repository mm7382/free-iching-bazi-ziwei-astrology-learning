## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Kui: Good fortune in small matters.

The Tuan commentary notes fire moving upward and the lake moving downward, two who share a dwelling and do not share a direction, which is why only small matters go well. It then adds that heaven and earth diverge yet their work is the same, man and woman diverge yet their wills connect, all things diverge yet their kinds correspond, so divergence has a great use. The Xiang commentary teaches sameness that leaves room for difference.

Dui below, Li above: fire rising, the lake sinking, two forces travelling away from each other, the image of disagreement and mutual mistrust. The judgement grants good fortune only in small matters, which tells you this is not a time for large undertakings and that a narrow scope goes more smoothly. The lines run from a lost horse that returns of itself and a meeting in a back lane, through a cart dragged back and an injury suffered, to finding a true ally in isolation, and finally from suspicion into a marriage proposal. The message is that divergence usually starts as misunderstanding, and once seen clearly, is rarely hostility.

The judgment limits good fortune to small matters, discouraging large undertakings. The third six's dragged wagon, restrained ox, and punished driver show hardship; the top nine first mistakes a suitor for a robber, reflecting repeated misunderstanding. Yet the first's vanished regret, the second's no blame, the fifth's blameless proceeding, and the top's fortune upon meeting rain all leave room to turn. The commentary also affirms Opposition's timely use. Overall this is a neutral situation in which measured action is possible. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: find the common ground first, then break the difference into smaller pieces and check that you each understand the other. Avoid: rushing to prove the other side wrong, or reading a temporary mismatch as opposition.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Li above, Dui below: a lake below fire — the two moving in different directions.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Kui.

- Explain why this arrangement reads as divergence.

- Point out that Divergence and Change use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Kui is Dui: two solid lines with a broken third. The upper is Li: solid, broken, solid. The first, second, fourth and sixth lines are solid.

Fire rises and a lake settles downward, so the two trigrams already point opposite ways. Structurally this reads as divergence: not that either side is wrong, but that they are heading differently. The hexagram does not argue for forced unity; it points out that where the divergence is clearly seen, partial cooperation remains possible.

A teaching example, not a prediction: two departments have genuinely different goals — one wants stability, the other speed — so every schedule has to be renegotiated. Kui describes that structure.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Nine at the beginning: Regret vanishes. Do not chase the lost horse; it returns by itself. Seeing an unpleasant person brings no blame.

The Xiang commentary says that meeting the person you dislike is a way of avoiding fault. The lost horse need not be chased, because it comes back of itself, and the estrangement is kept from deepening.

Regret dissolves. The horse that wandered off does not need chasing; it returns on its own. Even someone you dislike is worth seeing, and there is no fault in it. The point is neither to force things nor to close the door.

When someone has drifted away, you do not have to rush to win them back; time often brings them round. And keeping basic contact with people you do not click with stops a small coolness becoming a real problem.

That person you would rather not see, is it really hostility, or just a mismatch?

### Nine in the second place: Meeting the master in a narrow lane. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says that meeting the lord in a back lane has not departed from the way. It is an informal encounter, and in a time of divergence the value lies in being able to meet and connect at all.

You meet the person you needed to meet in a side street, and there is no fault. The commentary's phrase about not departing from the way means that informal, private conversation is not a breach of manners in a time of division. It is useful.

What will not move in a formal meeting often loosens over a coffee. Changing the channel does not mean you are going behind anyone's back.

Is there a lighter setting where this stuck conversation could happen?

### Six in the third place: Seeing a wagon dragged, its ox restrained, its driver branded on the forehead and nose cut off. No beginning, but an ending.

The Xiang commentary attributes the dragged cart to an unsuitable position, and says that a bad beginning with a good end comes from later meeting someone firm. The cart is held back, the ox is pulled aside, and the person suffers injury.

The cart is held back, the ox is dragged sideways, and the person is hurt. The start could hardly be rougher, yet the matter still comes to a result. The commentary puts the turn down to meeting someone firm, that is, finding the person who can support you.

Getting blocked at every step early on, and taking some unfairness with it, does not mean the thing cannot be done. Hold through this stretch and find the right partner, and it often turns around.

Is the resistance telling you the thing is wrong, or that your position is wrong?

### Nine in the fourth place: Isolated in opposition, meeting a worthy man, exchanging trust. Danger, but no blame.

The Xiang commentary says that meeting in sincerity and incurring no fault means the will can be carried out. Isolated by divergence, one meets an upright and good person.

In the middle of isolation you meet a straight and honest ally, and the two of you deal with each other in good faith, so despite the danger there is no fault. The answer to isolation is not compromise but finding the right person.

When a different position has left you isolated, do not force yourself to blend in. Find one or two people who genuinely understand you, and the work starts moving again.

Who around you actually understands what you are holding out for?

### Six in the fifth place: Regret vanishes. One's kin bites through soft flesh. What blame in proceeding?

The Xiang commentary says that when the kin bite through the soft flesh, going forward brings cause for celebration. Biting soft flesh is the figure for coming together with almost no resistance.

Regret dissolves, and joining with your own kin is as easy as biting through tender meat, so what fault could there be in going ahead? The divergence eases here, and work with people already on your side runs smoothly.

Start with the people whose position is closest to yours, where the resistance is lowest. Once that part is done, dealing with the harder side becomes far easier.

Have you been spending your effort on the hardest person to convince?

### Nine at the top: Isolated in opposition, seeing a mud-covered pig, a wagon full of ghosts. First drawing the bow, then releasing it. Not a robber, but a suitor. Meeting rain on the way brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary says the good fortune of meeting the rain lies in all the doubts dissolving. The mud-covered pig and the cartload of ghosts are apparitions produced by suspicion; the bow is drawn and then lowered, and the arrivals turn out to be suitors rather than bandits.

Isolation reaches its extreme and everything starts to look like a threat: a pig caked in mud, a cart loaded with ghosts. You draw the bow, then lower it, and find these are not raiders but a marriage party. The commentary is explicit that what actually dissolves is your own suspicion.

When it feels like everyone is against you, check whether that is fact or invention. Ask the question out loud, and most of the time you find there was no ill will at all.

Are you certain the other side is hostile, or have you simply not asked?

## Quick Quiz

- Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above)

- Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle)

- Qian · Heaven (three solid lines)

**Dui · Lake (two solid lines below, one broken above) (Correct)** : Correct. The first and second lines are solid; the third broken.

**Li · Fire (solid top and bottom, broken middle)** : Li is Kui's upper trigram.

**Qian · Heaven (three solid lines)** : Kui's third line is broken.

- Fire rises and the lake settles: the directions are already opposite

- Because someone made a mistake

- Because the halves are identical

**Fire rises and the lake descends; their directions are inherently opposite (Correct)** : Correct. These are the qualities of the two trigrams, not someone's mistake.

**Because someone made a mistake** : The hexagram describes different directions without judging right or wrong.

**Because the halves are identical** : Kui's trigrams are Li and Dui, which differ.

- See clearly where it lies; partial cooperation remains possible

- Everything must be unified into one direction

- Abandon cooperation altogether

**Recognizing the disagreement still allows cooperation in some areas (Correct)** : Correct. It does not advocate forcing everyone into agreement.

**Everything must be unified into one direction** : That is not what this structure describes.

**Abandon cooperation altogether** : The two trigrams still have adjacent solid lines, so cooperation is not wholly impossible.
