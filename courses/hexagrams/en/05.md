64 Hexagrams / 05

Wait in good faith; timing opens the way

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Xu: With sincerity, bright success. Steadfastness brings good fortune; crossing the great river is beneficial.

The Tuan commentary defines Xu as waiting, with danger ahead: firm and strong, one does not fall in, and so is not driven to extremity. The Xiang commentary sees clouds rising to heaven, and the noble person eating, drinking and keeping good spirits.

Xu is the image of moisture gathering in the sky over dry ground. The creative force meets danger blocking the way, yet does not sink into it; instead it waits with confidence intact. Holding a sound course through the wait leads to things opening up, and when the moment ripens, even a large crossing becomes reasonable. This is active waiting, not stalling.

Xu's judgment combines four positive statements: sincerity, bright success, steadfast good fortune, and beneficial river crossing. Those who maintain trust and constancy can ultimately progress through difficulty. Waiting is the main theme, but the overall trend is favorable, hence the rating: Highly Favorable.

Worth doing: use the waiting period to prepare, keep the commitments you have made, and watch for the turn. Avoid: hurrying the outcome, or acting while you still lack the information you need.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Kan above, Qian below: drive underneath, danger on top — stopped partway.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Xu.

- Explain why Qian below Kan reads as waiting.

- Point out that Waiting and Conflict use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Xu is Qian, three yang lines. The upper is Kan: one yang in the middle, yin on both sides. Read upward, the first three lines are full forward force, and at the fourth the figure enters Kan. The turn happens at the fourth line — the first line of the upper trigram.

Qian pushes forward and Kan is danger. Xu puts the drive below and the danger above, so the force is there but the road is not. This structure is read as waiting — yet waiting is not standing still: the Qian below is still active, preparation continues, it simply does not charge into the pit. Waiting and giving up are not the same thing.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a finished proposal held up by the other side's undecided timetable. Preparation carries on, but nobody is pressed to sign today. Xu describes that structure; it does not mean the deal will close.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Nine at the beginning: Waiting in the outskirts. Constancy is beneficial. No blame.

The Xiang commentary says waiting in the meadow means not walking into danger, and that constancy here keeps one within the ordinary course.

You are still at the safe outer edge, well clear of the risky centre. Keep your patience and your routine, do nothing rash, and nothing goes wrong.

Things are still forming. Do not rush to the front line — steady yourself at the back and wait for a clear signal.

Does your waiting have a rhythm to it, and what preparation keeps you from getting restless?

### Nine in the second place: Waiting on the sand. Some criticism; good fortune in the end.

The Xiang commentary says waiting on the sand keeps one broadly in the middle. Some words are exchanged, but holding the centre brings a good ending.

You are closer to the risky ground now. There is minor friction or sharp words, but keeping to a balanced course sees it end well.

Small obstacles or a bit of friction with people may unsettle you. It does not need much attention — hold your direction and carry on.

Can you tell which of the small irritations deserve attention and which can be let go?

### Nine in the third place: Waiting in the mud invites raiders.

The Xiang commentary says waiting in the mud puts the disaster outside; one has invited the trouble oneself, and only care and respect prevent defeat.

Impatience during the wait has already put you in a bind, and it has drawn in trouble you did not need. Careful handling matters more now than more pushing.

If haste got you into this, stop and deal with it carefully rather than grinding on or speeding up.

Did rushing put you where you are, and what is making it hard to step back out?

### Six in the fourth place: Waiting amid blood. Emerging from the pit.

The Xiang commentary says waiting amid blood means yielding and listening. From that place one comes out of the pit.

You are inside real danger, and this is not the moment to strike out. Go with the flow, listen, work with the situation, and you get clear of it.

When the position is genuinely risky, lowering your profile and fitting in with the current is the safe way out.

Do you need to drop the urge to act and follow the external rhythm instead?

### Nine in the fifth place: Waiting amid wine and food. Steadfastness brings good fortune.

The Xiang commentary attributes the good fortune of waiting over food and wine to holding the centre and staying correct.

From the most favourable position, waiting means enjoying the quiet pace you are in. A settled, balanced state is what makes this favourable.

No need for anxiety right now. Enjoy the calm you have and keep yourself steady inside.

Can you enjoy the present while waiting, rather than worrying at the future?

### Six at the top: Entering the pit, three uninvited guests arrive. Treat them with respect; good fortune in the end.

The Xiang commentary says that treating the uninvited guests with respect brings a good ending; the position is not ideal, but nothing much is lost.

In the last stage of waiting, something unexpected turns up. Meeting the unplanned person or event with respect produces a better ending than you expect.

Events may not go the way you pictured, and unfamiliar people or turns appear. Meet them openly and the result usually improves.

How do you meet unexpected turns and uninvited guests — with resistance or with openness?

## Quick Quiz

- The fourth, the first line of the upper Kan

- The third line

- The sixth line

**The fourth line, the first of upper Kan (Correct)** : Correct. The first three lines form Qian; the fourth begins Kan.

**The third line** : The third line still belongs to lower Qian and is solid.

**The sixth line** : The sixth is the upper trigram's last line; the transition occurs before it.

- There is drive below and danger above, so one does not charge in

- All six lines are yin, so nothing is done

- The halves are identical, so nothing can move

**Driving strength below, danger above: do not force your way through (Correct)** : Correct. Strength exists but a route does not; this structure is read as waiting.

**All six lines are yin, so nothing is done** : Six yin lines form Kun. Xu's lower three lines are all yang.

**The halves are identical, so nothing can move** : Xu's upper and lower trigrams are Kan and Qian, which differ.

- Sòng · Conflict

- Da You · Great Possession

- Bǐ · Holding Together

**Song · Conflict (Correct)** : Correct. Kan below and Qian above form Hexagram 6, Song: Xu's same two trigrams in exchanged positions.

**Da You · Great Possession** : Da You has Qian below and Li above; its upper trigram is not Kan.

**Bǐ · Holding Together** : Bi · Holding Together has Kun below and Kan above; its lower three lines are all broken.
