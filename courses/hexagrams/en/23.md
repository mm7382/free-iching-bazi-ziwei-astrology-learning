# Bo · Splitting Apart: Original Text, Plain-Language Explanation, and Six Lines

In decline, strengthen the base to secure the dwelling, stop without contention, and preserve the uneaten fruit

## Judgment and Plain-Language Notes

Bo: Unfavorable. There is somewhere to go.

Commentary on the Judgment: “Splitting Apart means stripping away: the yielding transforms the firm. Going anywhere is unfavorable because petty people grow. Receptively stop and observe the image. The noble person values waning and waxing, fullness and emptiness: Heaven's movement.” Commentary on the Image: “The mountain rests on Earth: Splitting Apart. Those above strengthen those below to secure their dwelling.” Five yin lines press upon one yang, explaining why advancing is unfavorable. The Image turns to governance: leaders must treat those below generously for their own dwelling to remain secure. (Zhouyi, Commentaries on the Judgment and the Image; public domain)

The mountain rests on the earth, and the ground beneath is being worn away layer by layer. Five yielding lines below and one firm line above make this the clearest picture of decline, which is why going forward is unfavourable. The lines strip the bed from its legs, then its frame, and finally reach the skin itself; each advance is dangerous. But the Xiang commentary offers the response: deal generously with those below and make the dwelling secure. The top line leaves one great fruit uneaten, so that even at the end of the stripping something can still grow.

The judgment explicitly discourages proceeding, explained by the growing influence of petty people. The first, second, and fourth sixes all declare misfortune, so the dominant tone is unfavorable. Yet the third six has no blame, the fifth nothing unfavorable, and the top nine preserves an uneaten great fruit as an opening for change. It is not total extinction, hence the rating: Unfavorable, rather than the lowest. This is the app's interpretation of the text, not a guarantee of outcomes.

Worth doing: reduce the drain, protect what is fundamental, and stop the commitments that are working against you. Avoid: expanding against the current, straining to save face, or giving up the core resources along with the rest.

## Understand This Hexagram Through Its Structure

Gen above, Kun below: the one solid line stands alone at the top over five yin lines.

- Name the upper and lower trigram of Bo.

- Explain why a lone solid line at the top reads as stripping away.

- Point out that Stripping Away and Modesty use the same two trigrams in the other order.

The lower trigram of Bo is Kun, all broken; the upper is Gen, broken at the fourth and fifth and solid at the sixth. Only the sixth line of the figure is solid; the five beneath it are broken.

Yin lines have grown from the bottom all the way to the fifth place, leaving only the topmost solid line. Structurally this reads as being stripped layer by layer: what remains sits highest and is supported least. This is not a statement about disaster but about proportion — the base has already turned over, and how long the top layer holds is a structural question.

A teaching example, not a prediction: a legacy system has one engineer left who still knows it, everyone else having moved on. Bo describes that structure of thinning support; it does not say the system fails tomorrow.

## The Six Lines: Original Text and Reflection

### Six at the beginning: Stripping the bed at its legs. Disregarding steadfastness brings misfortune.

The bed is stripped at the legs. The Xiang commentary says the foundation is being destroyed; treating the straight path with contempt because the damage is out of sight is what plants the misfortune.

The stripping begins at the legs of the bed, in the lowest and least visible place. Treating what is straight with contempt because you cannot see the damage is where the danger takes root.

Problems usually show up first in the foundations: cash flow, health habits, trust. Repair the base while the loss is still small, rather than leaving it because the surface still holds.

Where in your foundations has something already started to loosen?

### Six in the second place: Stripping the bed at its frame. Disregarding steadfastness brings misfortune.

The bed is stripped at the frame, closer now to the person. The Xiang commentary notes the absence of any answering support, so contempt for the straight path again brings misfortune.

The stripping moves up to where the bed joins together, closer to the person on it. There is no answering support anywhere, which is what makes disregarding the straight path dangerous here.

When the losses widen and no support is at hand, ask for help and describe the situation to someone you trust. Do not hold out alone until there is no room left to manoeuvre.

Have you told anyone what is actually happening?

### Six in the third place: Stripping away. No blame.

Stripping away, yet without blame. The Xiang commentary attributes this to keeping a distance from the lines above and below: not decaying along with everyone else preserves this one.

This line sits inside the same decline yet takes no blame, because it has put distance between itself and those on either side. Not eroding along with everyone else is what saves it.

When the whole environment is sliding, you can still decline to cut corners or give up along with it. Keeping your distance from a bad culture may spare you from being dragged into it.

Are you willing to be a little out of step in order to hold your principles?

### Six in the fourth place: Stripping the bed reaches the skin. Misfortune.

The stripping reaches the skin: misfortune. The Xiang commentary says calamity is close at hand. This line has no buffer left and must meet the matter directly.

The stripping spreads from the bed to the skin. This is no longer damage to an object but harm to the person. There is no buffer left, only the thing itself to face.

When the damage has reached your health, your livelihood or the relationship itself, stop the loss at once and get outside help. Deal with the most immediate thing first and set the rest aside.

If you could only save one thing first, what would it be?

### Six in the fifth place: A string of fish. Favor through the palace attendants. Nothing unfavorable.

Fish strung in a line, palace attendants cared for in order: nothing is unfavourable. The Xiang commentary says no fault remains, since orderly places for everyone hold the situation during a decline.

Like fish strung in a row, everyone is attended to in turn. During a decline, giving each person a clear place in the order is what keeps things from going wrong.

When resources are tight, set out a clear order and a rule for distribution so everyone knows when their turn comes. That steadies things far better than letting people compete for what is left.

Have you made the order of priority for your limited resources explicit?

### Nine at the top: A great fruit remains uneaten. The noble person gains a carriage; the petty person's dwelling is stripped away.

One great fruit is left uneaten. The Xiang commentary separates two endings: the one who deals generously with those below is carried by them, while the one who only strips loses even the roof overhead.

At the end of the stripping, one large fruit is left uneaten, and with it the chance to grow again. Two endings part here: the generous are carried by others, while those who only take lose even their own roof.

In the hardest stretch, keep the one thing that matters most, whether that is your credit, your core skill or a particular relationship. Treat the people still supporting you well and do not burn the last of what you have.

Which fruit is the one you must not eat, whatever happens?
