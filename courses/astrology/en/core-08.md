THE IDEA

The outer planets move very slowly and describe the backdrop your generation shares.

Table: Uranus stays in a sign about seven years, Neptune about fourteen and Pluto about twenty. All averages from NASA orbit periods; retrograde motion changes any single pass.

Your chart has ten bodies; the outer three were found with telescopes.

## The outer three

All three were found only once there were telescopes (NASA): Uranus was discovered in 1781 by William Herschel, the first planet found with a telescope; Neptune was discovered in 1846; Pluto was discovered in 1930 and reclassified as a dwarf planet by the International Astronomical Union in 2006. So your chart holds ten bodies in all: the Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, which the eye can see, plus these three outer planets.

The outer planets are slow and far away, so read them as the backdrop: the first seven describe how you yourself think, love and push, and the outer three describe what the times you live in are pushing for.

Open your chart and count: read the first seven to see yourself clearly, then come back to where the outer three fall and think about what your generation has been through together.

What the old texts say: in Book I ch. 4 of the Tetrabiblos, Ptolemy sets out each planet’s nature one by one and names seven — Sun, Moon, Saturn, Mars, Jupiter, Venus and Mercury. Ch. 5 sorts the same seven into beneficent and maleficent. These seven are all the planets visible to the eye.

An outer planet takes seven to twenty years to change sign.

## Slow enough to mean a generation

From NASA figures, Uranus goes round the Sun in about 30,685.400 days, Neptune in about 60,189.018 days and Pluto in about 90,560 days. Shared across twelve signs, that is roughly seven, fourteen and twenty years a sign. NASA also records that the International Astronomical Union reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet in 2006.

At that pace, one sign holds a whole generation. So an outer planet’s sign describes the times a generation shares: Uranus in a given sign, for instance, describes the mood shared by everyone born in those years. Getting down to you takes the house it fell in on your chart, and houses need a birth time.

What the old texts say: Ptolemy’s planets end at Saturn; Book I ch. 4 of the Tetrabiblos calls Saturn the one furthest from the Sun’s heat.

Read the outer planets as your generation’s backdrop.

## So how are they used

Uranus is about breaking with convention, Neptune about things with blurred edges (imagination, and self-deception too), and Pluto about thorough change. Take Uranus: ask which convention your generation is most used to breaking.

A useful way to read them is as the questions your generation faced together, and then look at how you yourself answered.

This app computes the positions of all three and shows them on your chart as they are, so you can see where your generation falls.

What the old texts say: when Book I ch. 5 of the Tetrabiblos sorts beneficent from maleficent, it too uses the seven planets visible to the eye.

## An illustration

Two people a year apart almost certainly share all three outer signs.

Yet the youngest child and the eldest answer the same era’s questions in completely different ways.

So the outer planets set the question; what makes each person different shows up elsewhere.

LOOK CLOSER

## A common mix-up

Pluto changes sign about every twenty years, so a placement is shared by a whole generation and describes that generation’s backdrop.

One Uranus sign is shared by a whole generation and describes the question that generation faces; how you yourself answer shows in the first seven bodies and in the house it falls in on your chart.

The 2006 reclassification is about how astronomy sorts things. Its position is shown as computed, and you read it the same way as the other two: as a generation’s backdrop.
