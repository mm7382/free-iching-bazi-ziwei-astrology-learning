# What are yin and yang? The basic unit of the I Ching explained

> This is a mirror. The **canonical, most up-to-date version** is at https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/yin-and-yang.html

Written by [Wei Chun Chuang](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/about.html)・2026-11-24

*Yin and yang are the smallest unit of the I Ching: an unbroken line is yang, a broken line is yin. What the words originally meant, why they are relative rather than good and bad, what the two dots in the taiji symbol mean, how lines build into trigrams and hexagrams, and three common misunderstandings.*

---

The first thing to learn about the I Ching is not the sixty-four hexagrams but two kinds of line. An unbroken horizontal line is a **yang line**; a line broken in the middle is a **yin line**. The whole book — sixty-four hexagrams, three hundred and eighty-four lines — is built from these two. Get this layer clear and everything after it is easier.

## What the words originally meant

Yin and yang first described the two sides of a hill: the side facing the sun is yang, the side turned away is yin. One hill, one moment, one side lit and one side dark. That picture is the original sense.

Three things follow from it. First, yin and yang are **two sides of one thing**, not two separate things. Second, they are **relative**: away from that hill the words mean nothing. Third, they **swap over time**, because the sun moves and the lit side goes dark. Every later extension grows from these three.

## Not good and bad

This is the most common misunderstanding. Yang does not mean good, strong, male or positive; yin does not mean bad, weak, female or negative. In the language of the I Ching, yang tends toward the active, the expanding and the visible, and yin toward the receptive, the gathering and the hidden. That is a **classification of quality**, not a ranking of worth.

In fact one of the most favourably read hexagrams is Kun, which is yin in all six lines; while Qian is yang in all six, and its top line reads “a dragon that overreaches has cause for regret”. All-yang is not better than all-yin. Excess is the problem.

Yin and yang are also **relative judgements**, not fixed labels. Day is yang next to night, but morning is yin next to noon. Asking whether something “is yin or yang” means nothing until you ask: relative to what?

## The two dots in the taiji symbol

Everyone has seen the black-and-white circle. Two details are worth noticing:

- **The halves are divided by an S-curve, not a straight line.** Yin and yang wax and wane into each other; this is a process, not a cut between two blocks. As one grows the other shrinks.

- **The white half holds a black dot and the black half a white dot.** There is yin within yang and yang within yin. No state is pure, which is also why the I Ching never reads any hexagram as absolutely fortunate or unfortunate.

## From two lines to sixty-four hexagrams

There are only two steps:

- **Stack three lines to get a trigram.** Each line is yin or yang, so there are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 combinations: Qian, Dui, Li, Zhen, Xun, Kan, Gen and Kun.

- **Stack two trigrams to get a hexagram.** 8 × 8 = 64. Every hexagram therefore splits into an upper and a lower trigram: Approach, for example, is Kun above and Dui below.

The six lines are counted from the bottom up: the lowest is the first line, the highest is the top line. Direction matters, because the I Ching tells its story upward, like something moving from beginning to end.

## Three misunderstandings

- **“Yin and yang should be balanced fifty-fifty.”** The I Ching talks about being in the right place at the right time, not about equal halves. Some situations call for yang to dominate, some for yin. All-yin Kun is not out of balance.

- **“Yang is male and yin is female.”** That is one later correspondence, not the definition. The same pair describes motion and rest, firm and yielding, day and night, advance and retreat; forcing it onto gender makes most of the line texts unreadable.

- **“Yin and yang are superstition.”** They are **a vocabulary for describing change**, not a causal claim about nature. Used as a way of classifying, they are fine. Used as a way of predicting, they are being asked for more than they can carry.

## How to practise

Take a sheet of paper, draw six lines, and decide at random whether each is broken or unbroken. Split the result into an upper and a lower trigram and look up which of the sixty-four hexagrams it is. Do that five times and you will never again wonder how two kinds of line become a hexagram.

## Common questions

**What do yin and yang actually mean?**

They first described the two sides of a hill: the sunlit side is yang, the shaded side is yin. Three features follow — they are two sides of one thing, they only mean something relative to a reference point, and they swap over time. In the I Ching an unbroken line is yang and a broken line is yin.

**Is yang good and yin bad?**

No. Yang tends toward the active, expanding and visible; yin toward the receptive, gathering and hidden. This classifies quality, not worth. All-yin Kun is read favourably, while all-yang Qian ends with a dragon that overreaches and has cause for regret.

**What do the two dots in the taiji symbol mean?**

The white half holds a black dot and the black half a white dot: there is yin within yang and yang within yin, and no state is pure. The S-curve between the halves shows that one waxes as the other wanes, rather than the two being cut apart.

**How do yin and yang build into the sixty-four hexagrams?**

Three lines stacked give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 trigrams; two trigrams stacked give 8 × 8 = 64 hexagrams. Every hexagram therefore splits into an upper and a lower trigram, and the six lines are counted from the bottom up.
