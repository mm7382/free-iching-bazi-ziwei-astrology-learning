# What do the brightness levels of stars mean in Zi Wei Dou Shu?

Looking at a Zi Wei Dou Shu chart for the first time, you will notice a small label next to each star name: Miao, Wang, Li, Ping, Xian. This set of labels is usually called the star's brightness, or its Miao-Wang grading. This article explains what it describes, which grades are commonly used, why books disagree, and how a beginner should read it.

## What is being graded?

Each of the twelve palaces corresponds to an earthly branch. Traditionally, a star sits better in some branches than in others: some let its described qualities open out, others constrain them. Earlier readers recorded that degree as a grade, and those records are the brightness tables.

In other words, brightness describes how fully the qualities attributed to a star are expected to show. It does not describe whether something is good or bad. It is a scale of degree, not a scale of fortune.

## Which grades are commonly used?

One commonly used set, from highest to lowest:

- **Miao 廟** — best placed; the qualities show most fully.

- **Wang 旺** — well placed.

- **De Di 得地**, written **Li 利** in some books — adequate.

- **Ping 平** — ordinary, unremarkable.

- **Bu De Di 不得地** — constrained.

- **Xian 陷** — least well placed; the qualities are harder to express fully.

Note that the number of grades and their names are not standardised. Some transmitted versions use only four (Miao, Wang, Li, Xian); others use six or seven, and "De Di" and "Li" do not always refer to the same step from one book to the next. When you see a label on a chart, check which scale it belongs to.

## Xian is not a bad omen, and Miao is not a guarantee

This is worth stating on its own. Xian is often misread as "this palace is bad", but on its own terms it says that in this position the star has more trouble expressing its qualities fully: it may read as effort that does not land, as larger swings, as taking the long way round. That is a description, not an outcome.

Equally, Miao does not mean something will succeed. A star expressing itself fully also expresses its excesses fully. Reading brightness as degree rather than as a score stays closer to how it was used.

## Where do the tables come from?

These tables come from the classical Zi Wei Dou Shu texts and manuscript copies, each adjusted as it was handed down, and they do not agree with one another. The same star in the same branch may be graded differently in different books, and some books do not grade certain stars at all. The disagreement is genuine, not a matter of someone miscalculating. Chart software prints whichever table it follows, so different tools can show different labels.

## How should a beginner read it?

Treat it as the third clue:

- First, which palace is this (which area of life is under discussion)?

- Then, which star is in it (what qualities are described)?

- Last, the brightness (how fully those qualities are expected to show).

Brightness adds emphasis; on its own it carries no content. Drawing a conclusion from the single word Xian means skipping the first two steps.

## FAQ

**Does a star in Xian mean something bad?**

No. Xian describes a star whose described qualities are expected to show less fully. It is not a bad omen, and Miao is not a guarantee. Brightness speaks to degree of expression, not to outcome.

**Why do books disagree about brightness grades?**

The grading tables come from different classical texts, and the number and names of the grades were never standardised; some use four levels, others six or seven. The same star in the same branch can be graded differently from one book to the next.

**Do beginners need to memorise the tables?**

No. Chart tools print the grade for you. At the start it is enough to recognise the labels, know they describe degree of expression, and read them alongside the palace and the star itself.
