The I Ching (Book of Changes) can look intimidating, but its structure is very regular: two kinds of line form eight three-line trigrams, and pairs of trigrams stacked together form sixty-four hexagrams. If you learn in that order, you can soon see how any hexagram is built. This article walks you through the first steps.

## What kind of book is the I Ching?

"I Ching" usually refers to the Zhou Yi, which has two parts: the classic and the commentaries. The classic contains the sixty-four hexagram figures, their names, the judgment for each hexagram and the statements for each line. The commentaries are ten later texts explaining the classic, known together as the "Ten Wings", such as the Tuan, the Xiang and the Great Commentary (Xici).

Beginners do best by understanding the structure of the classic first and consulting the commentaries gradually.

## How do you tell a yin line from a yang line?

The smallest unit is the line. A solid line "⚊" is yang; a broken line "⚋" is yin.

Yin and yang are not good and bad. They are a pair of relative ideas: firm and yielding, moving and still, bright and dark. Seeing whether each line is yin or yang is where every reading begins.

## Where do the eight trigrams come from?

Stack three lines from the bottom up. Each line can be yin or yang, so there are eight combinations (2×2×2 = 8): the eight trigrams. Each has a name and a familiar image:

- Qian ☰ — Heaven

- Kun ☷ — Earth

- Zhen ☳ — Thunder

- Xun ☴ — Wind

- Kan ☵ — Water

- Li ☲ — Fire

- Gen ☶ — Mountain

- Dui ☱ — Lake

Learn these symbols and images well, because every one of the sixty-four hexagrams is made from them.

## How are the sixty-four hexagrams formed?

Put one trigram on top of another and you get a six-line hexagram. Eight times eight gives sixty-four. The bottom three lines are the lower (inner) trigram; the top three are the upper (outer) trigram.

For example, Water (Kan) above Fire (Li) is "After Completion" (Ji Ji); turn it around, Fire above Water, and you get "Before Completion" (Wei Ji), the last of the sixty-four. Many full hexagram names simply combine the images of the two trigrams with the hexagram's name.

## Where do you start reading a hexagram?

Lines are counted from the bottom up: the first (bottom) line, then lines two to five, and the top line. Yang lines are called "nine" and yin lines "six", so "first nine" means the bottom line is yang and "top six" means the top line is yin.

Each hexagram has one judgment for the whole figure, followed by six line statements. Qian and Kun each have one extra statement, "using nines" and "using sixes".

## What is a good learning order for beginners?

- Tell yin lines from yang lines.

- Learn the symbols and images of the eight trigrams.

- Practise splitting each hexagram into its upper and lower trigrams.

- Read the judgment to grasp the overall idea.

- Read the line statements and notice how the situation changes from bottom to top.

- When you are ready, consult the Tuan and Xiang commentaries.

One hexagram a day is plenty. The goal is not to memorise every word but to understand the structure, so that you can take any hexagram apart and explain it.

## FAQ

**Do I need to cast hexagrams to learn the I Ching?**

No. You can read the I Ching as a classic text and start with yin and yang, the trigrams and the line structure. Casting is one way to meet a hexagram, best used as a prompt for self-reflection rather than something that makes decisions for you.

**Do I have to memorise all sixty-four hexagrams?**

Not all at once. Once you know the eight trigrams, you can understand any hexagram as an upper and a lower half; names and judgments stick naturally with repeated reading.

**Are the I Ching and the eight trigrams the same thing?**

The trigrams are the building blocks. The body of the I Ching is the sixty-four hexagrams formed from pairs of trigrams, together with the texts that explain them.
