## The year’s theme: keep what you build, and make things clear

You do not have to reinvent your whole life at once. What efforts have you made without organising, showing or sustaining them? What repeatedly drains you but has never been discussed clearly?

We translate the Ding year’s four transformations into four everyday themes: Tai Yin to Lu, building resources; Tian Tong to Quan, setting boundaries around comfort and responsibility; Tian Ji to Ke, organising methods and expertise; and Ju Men to Ji, noticing misunderstandings and unchecked statements. This is our modern interpretation, not a claim that these events must happen in 2027.

**Take one thought with you: care for what you already have before deciding what to pursue next.**

## Four stars, four annual reminders

### Tai Yin transforms to Lu | Notice what grows over time

Tai Yin is often associated with care, resources and gradual accumulation. Read its Lu transformation through the question: what is worth nurturing? That may be money, but also trustworthy relationships, reliable methods and a steadier daily life.

For example: rather than choosing a different side business every week, review what your existing clients ask for most. Make one thing you already do dependable.

### Tian Tong transforms to Quan | Care for yourself and take the lead

Pairing Tian Tong’s comfort imagery with Quan’s responsibility invites active choices rather than endless endurance. Schedule rest, and say clearly how much help you can offer.

For example: when a colleague asks you to take on another urgent task, clarify the priority and deadline first. Being helpful does not require accepting everything.

### Tian Ji transforms to Ke | Make your methods visible

Turn flexible thinking into methods others can understand. Alongside learning a tool, learn how to check its output and help someone else continue the work.

For example: after learning a tool, create a real project or a set of process notes. On your CV, explain the problem you solved, not simply that you know the tool.

### Ju Men transforms to Ji | Check important words once more

The language imagery associated with Ju Men is used here as a reminder to manage misunderstandings. Concern about mistakes need not become suspicion of everyone. Clarify conditions, responsibilities and anything uncertain.

For example: when someone says “probably this week”, ask whether they mean a first draft by Friday or everything finished. Help both people understand what was actually agreed.

## Money | Keep what you build

This money chapter begins with Tai Yin’s Lu transformation, focusing on organising and retaining resources. Look at where money comes from and where it goes before deciding which income source or skill to develop.

A popular opportunity may not suit your time, experience or capacity for risk. “Everyone is doing it” is not a reason you must follow. An annual reading cannot establish that an industry or investment will make money.

Try: review regular income and expenses, including unused subscriptions. Confirm terms and the other party’s identity before a financial arrangement.

Be mindful: chasing quick money out of anxiety, treating personal goodwill as a substitute for clear terms, or believing guaranteed returns.

When things get difficult: list known expenses and obligations. Pause and check unfamiliar transactions; avoid rushing into another commitment to recover a loss.

This week: find one expense you no longer need and decide whether to keep, adjust or stop it.

## Work | Make skills visible and responsibilities clear

Tian Ji’s Ke transformation offers a theme of methods and learning; Tian Tong’s Quan transformation draws attention to roles and responsibilities. At work, that means giving your skills evidence and your commitments a clear scope.

If you are considering a change of job, review your achievements and skills to strengthen. If you are staying, clarify your priorities. This chapter cannot decide whether you should leave or stay, and a year does not guarantee promotion.

Try: organise your work samples, write down delivery criteria and learn one genuinely useful tool. Check AI-generated work yourself.

Be mindful: verbally accepting work you cannot finish, collecting fashionable skill names, or handing over unverified results.

When things get difficult: replace “I am busy” with specific tasks and deadlines, then ask the people involved to agree on priorities.

This week: describe one recent achievement in three sentences: the original problem, your action and the actual result.

## Relationships | Kindness can have boundaries

This chapter brings Tai Yin’s care imagery together with Ju Men’s communication reminder. A relationship needs not only willingness to give, but also a shared understanding of what is happening.

With a partner, discuss responsibilities, money and time together. If you are single, notice actions as well as appealing words. No star makes someone destined to be right or wrong for you.

Try: state your needs directly, ask how the other person is doing and keep promises consistent with actions.

Be mindful: testing someone with silence, relying on guesses, or repeatedly giving up your needs to avoid disagreement.

When things get difficult: separate what happened from your assumptions, then agree on a time to talk. If you face threats or harm, prioritise safety and seek help.

This week: replace “You never understand me” with one specific request the other person can respond to.

## Health | Make room to care for yourself

Drawing on Tian Tong’s imagery of rest, this chapter invites you to notice whether life leaves too little room to recover. General transformations cannot establish health risks, and an annual reading cannot diagnose an organ problem or illness.

Try: make room for sustainable rest, regular routines, balanced meals and activity appropriate to your health.

Be mindful: replacing healthcare with lucky objects, stopping medication because of a reading, or treating every symptom as an effect of the year.

When things get difficult: seek a qualified healthcare professional for persistent or concerning symptoms. Do not wait for a supposedly favourable month.

This week: protect some time away from work and notice which everyday arrangements leave you persistently tired.

## Lucky colours | A visible reminder for the year

These three colours are symbolic styling suggestions for this feature, not universal lucky colours calculated from a Zi Wei chart. There is no evidence that wearing them changes wealth or health.

### Moonlight Silver | Give yourself a little steadiness

Echoing Tai Yin and moonlight, an existing silver accessory or light-coloured notebook can remind you to find a calmer pace before responding.

### Deep Ocean Blue | Think first, then speak

Use this quiet water colour as a communication reminder. Before an important meeting, review your notes and list the questions you need answered.

### Soft Gold | Notice what you have already done

A visual symbol of accumulation and achievements. Mark each small completed goal on your calendar. You do not need to buy anything for better luck.

## Coping practice | Break difficulty into manageable steps

“Turning difficulty around” means practical coping exercises here. It does not promise to avert disaster or change fate.

### Step 1: Pause

When a message excites, frightens or pressures you to respond, separate facts from assumptions. The more urgently someone demands a decision, the more important it is to check the information.

### Step 2: Clarify

Ask who is responsible, when something will be finished and what limits apply. Many everyday conflicts begin with each person assuming the other already knows.

### Step 3: Write it down

For money, work or shared plans, keep a record that both sides understand in the same way. When specialist judgement is needed, consult the appropriate professional.

## A practical rhythm for the whole year

This is a personal action plan you can arrange for yourself, not a calculation of lucky or unlucky months.

January–March: take stock. Review resources, income, expenses and the parts of work or daily life that consume the most energy.

April–June: practise. Choose a skill worth developing and keep evidence of what you achieve.

July–September: look back. Check what is working and what needs adjustment; do not assume that the second half of the year guarantees a reward.

October–December: preserve. Gather the work, methods and relationships you have developed and decide what to continue next year.

## Bring the reminders into your life

Choose one small thing you can do today: review an expense, clarify a responsibility, finish a piece of work or make time to rest. You do not need to change everything after reading. Begin with one thing.
