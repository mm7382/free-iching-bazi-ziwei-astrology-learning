# What does the Kun hexagram mean? The second hexagram of the I Ching in plain words

Kun is the second of the sixty-four hexagrams, immediately after [Qian](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/qian-hexagram.html). All six lines are yin and both trigrams are Kun, so its full name is "Kun is Earth". This article explains its text line by line in plain words.

## What does Kun look like?

Six broken yin lines: Kun ☷ above and Kun ☷ below. The trigram's image is earth and its quality is yielding — carrying, going along with things, not pushing to be first.

Its counterpart is Qian, six yang lines, the image of heaven and active strength. Qian initiates and Kun carries; the two are often treated as the starting point of the whole book. For the symbols themselves, see [the eight trigrams](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/eight-trigrams.html).

## What does the judgment say?

Kun's judgment runs: great success; it is favourable to be steadfast like a mare. The noble one has somewhere to go: at first they lose the way, later they find their bearings. Going south-west brings companions, going north-east loses them. Settled steadfastness brings good fortune. Phrase by phrase:

- Great success: a good beginning that gets through. The opening matches Qian's.

- Steadfast like a mare: a mare covers long distances and is gentle — the fitting kind of steadiness here.

- First lose the way, later find your bearings: direction is not obvious at the start.

- South-west and north-east: a symbolic reading of the directions; tradition treats south-west as yin and north-east as yang.

- Settled steadfastness brings good fortune: staying correct and unhurried works out.

The gist: yielding does not mean having no direction. It means moving in a lasting, settled way without grabbing the lead.

## What do the six lines say?

The six lines follow a yielding force from its first sign, through its useful work, to going too far. "Six" in a line name means a yin line; first, second and so on to top give its position from the bottom.

- First six: "Treading on frost. Hard ice is coming." Act on the small early sign.

- Second six: "Straight, square, great. Without practice, nothing is unfavourable." Often read as Kun's best position.

- Third six: "Keeping your brilliance contained, you may stay steadfast. Serving in public affairs, claim no credit and it still ends well."

- Fourth six: "A tied-up sack. No blame, no praise." Close the bag when it is time to close it.

- Fifth six: "A yellow lower garment. Fundamentally auspicious." Yellow is the colour of the centre and the garment is worn below: holding an honoured place while keeping to your part.

- Top six: "Dragons fight in the open field; their blood is dark and yellow." Yielding taken to its extreme turns into conflict.

## What is "using sixes"?

Kun has one extra statement: "It is favourable to be lastingly steadfast." Qian has a matching "using nines"; only these two hexagrams carry such a line.

## Where does "ample virtue carries all things" come from?

From Kun's Image commentary: the form of the earth is yielding, and the noble one likewise carries all things with ample virtue. It is often quoted alongside Qian's "strengthens themselves without ceasing"; Tsinghua University's motto is drawn from the pair.

## What can you learn from Kun?

Kun makes a good exercise in carrying and supporting. Is your situation more like noticing a first sign, working steadily, holding your talent back, keeping the bag shut, holding a place while keeping to your part, or already locked in conflict? Read that way, the hexagram is more useful than a verdict of good or bad luck. To find a hexagram by name, see [how to look one up](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/hexagram-lookup.html).

## FAQ

**Is Kun a good hexagram?**

Its judgment is broadly favourable, but the first line warns to read small signs early and the top line describes conflict when yin reaches its extreme. Every hexagram mixes favourable conditions with cautions, so good-or-bad is not a useful way to read it.

**What does "treading on frost, hard ice is coming" mean?**

Frost underfoot tells you the hard freeze is on its way. Situations announce themselves early; act on the small signs rather than waiting until things have set.

**How do Qian and Kun differ?**

Qian is six yang lines, the image of heaven and active strength. Kun is six yin lines, the image of earth: carrying, supporting and holding steady. The pair is often treated as the starting point of the whole book.
