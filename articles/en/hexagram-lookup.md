# How do you look up one of the sixty-four hexagrams?

Once you have six lines, you need to know which of the sixty-four hexagrams they are. You do not have to memorise sixty-four figures: you split the lines into two trigrams and look them up. This article covers the split, how to identify each trigram, how to use the table, and how to find the line statements afterwards.

## A hexagram is two trigrams stacked

Each of the sixty-four hexagrams is built from two three-line trigrams, one on top of the other. The bottom three lines are the lower, or inner, trigram; the top three are the upper, or outer, trigram. Eight trigrams in each position give eight times eight, which is exactly sixty-four.

## How do you split six lines into two trigrams?

Just cut the stack in the order it was drawn:

- Confirm the lines run from the bottom upward, so the lowest is the first line.

- Lines one, two and three together are the lower trigram.

- Lines four, five and six together are the upper trigram.

- Write down which trigram is below and which is above before you touch the table.

The order is not interchangeable. Kan below with Li above and Li below with Kan above are two different hexagrams with different names and different texts.

## How do you identify a trigram from its three lines?

Each of the three lines is yin or yang, giving eight combinations. Read them from the bottom up:

- Qian: all three yang

- Kun: all three yin

- Zhen: yang, yin, yin

- Gen: yin, yin, yang

- Li: yang, yin, yang

- Kan: yin, yang, yin

- Dui: yang, yang, yin

- Xun: yin, yang, yang

A shortcut once you are used to them: the three trigrams with a single yang line are Zhen, Kan and Gen, with that line at the bottom, middle and top; the three with a single yin line are Xun, Li and Dui, in the same order.

## How do you use the 8x8 table?

The standard lookup table is a grid of eight columns and eight rows: one axis lists the eight lower trigrams, the other lists the eight upper trigrams, and the cell where they meet is the hexagram, usually printed with its name and its number. Find the band for your lower trigram, then follow it to your upper trigram. The one real pitfall is reading the axes the wrong way round, so check the labels first.

## How does the received sequence differ from the table?

The received text of the I Ching arranges the sixty-four hexagrams in a fixed traditional order, usually called the King Wen sequence, beginning with Qian and Kun and ending with Ji Ji and Wei Ji, with neighbouring hexagrams often forming pairs. That order is not produced mechanically from the trigrams, so the cells of an 8x8 table do not run in numerical order. Hexagrams are normally cited as a number plus a name, for example "1 Qian" or "63 Ji Ji". The numbering is broadly consistent across editions, but names and translations differ, so it helps to say which edition you are using.

## Finding the line statements

Each hexagram has a statement for the hexagram as a whole plus six line statements, numbered by position from the bottom up: first, second, third, fourth, fifth and top. Once you know the hexagram's name you can turn to it and read the statement for any position. If your casting produced changing lines, read those positions; if it produced none, read the hexagram text and its image alone.

## FAQ

**Can I mix up the upper and lower trigram?**

Yes, and swapping them gives a different hexagram. Kan below with Li above is not the same hexagram as Li below with Kan above. Check which axis of the table you are reading.

**Is the hexagram number the same as the table order?**

No. The received text has its own fixed sequence of the sixty-four hexagrams, which differs from the order an 8x8 table produces. The table is a finding aid; the sequence is a separate traditional arrangement.

**How do I read the line statements once I have the hexagram?**

Line statements are numbered by position, from the bottom upward: first, second, third, fourth, fifth and top. Once you know which lines are changing, read the statements for those positions.
