# What are the sixty-four hexagrams? Names, trigrams and order explained

Open the I Ching and the first thing you meet is sixty-four figures made of six horizontal lines each, called hexagrams. There seem to be a lot of them, but they come from one simple rule: the eight basic trigrams stacked in pairs. This article explains how hexagrams are built, how their names work and how they are ordered.

## Where do the sixty-four hexagrams come from?

The I Ching uses only two kinds of line: a solid yang line ⚊ and a broken yin line ⚋. Stack three and you get 2×2×2 = 8 combinations, the [eight trigrams](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/eight-trigrams.html): Qian ☰, Dui ☱, Li ☲, Zhen ☳, Xun ☴, Kan ☵, Gen ☶ and Kun ☷.

Stack two trigrams, one above the other, and you get 8×8 = 64 combinations: the sixty-four hexagrams. Each has six lines, 384 lines in all.

## Which part is the upper and which the lower trigram?

Lines are counted from the bottom up: first line, second, third, fourth, fifth and top line. The bottom three form the lower (inner) trigram and the top three the upper (outer) trigram.

Readers usually identify the two trigrams first and then look at the whole. Water above and thunder below, for example, combine into the image of that hexagram.

## How do you read a name like "Water Thunder Zhun"?

A common full name has two parts: the nature images of the upper and lower trigrams, then the hexagram's own name.

- Water Thunder Zhun: Kan (water) above, Zhen (thunder) below; the name is Zhun, "Difficulty at the Beginning".

- Heaven Earth Pi: Qian (heaven) above, Kun (earth) below; the name is Pi, "Standstill".

- Earth Heaven Tai: Kun above, Qian below; the name is Tai, "Peace".

When both trigrams are the same, the name says so, such as "Qian is Heaven" or "Kan is Water". There are eight of these doubled hexagrams.

Pi and Tai are the same two trigrams turned upside down, yet they are different hexagrams with almost opposite meanings, so always keep track of which trigram is on top.

## What order are the hexagrams in?

The received text of the Zhou Yi divides the hexagrams into an Upper Canon of thirty (Qian to Li) and a Lower Canon of thirty-four (Xian to Wei Ji). The Sequence of the Hexagrams commentary explains the order step by step. The Song-dynasty scholar Zhu Xi recorded a short rhyme listing all sixty-four names in order, and Chinese learners still use it to memorise them.

## Is there a pattern in the order?

Most hexagrams come in pairs. Usually the second of a pair is the first turned upside down; Zhun inverted is Meng. Eight hexagrams look the same upside down, so they are paired by swapping every yin and yang line instead: Qian and Kun, Yi and Da Guo, Kan and Li, Zhong Fu and Xiao Guo.

Seen this way, you are learning thirty-two pairs rather than sixty-four separate names.

## Should beginners memorise all sixty-four?

Not at first. An easier route is to learn the eight trigrams, then practise splitting any hexagram into its upper and lower trigrams, and only then learn the names and order. Once you can split a hexagram, even an unfamiliar one tells you its two images. See [how to look up a hexagram](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/hexagram-lookup.html).

## FAQ

**How many lines are there in all?**

Six per hexagram, 384 in total. Qian and Kun each also have an extra statement, "using nines" and "using sixes".

**What do the first two words in a name mean?**

They are the images of the upper and then the lower trigram. Water Thunder Zhun has Kan (water) above and Zhen (thunder) below.

**How are the Upper and Lower Canons divided?**

In the received text the Upper Canon is hexagrams 1 to 30 (Qian to Li) and the Lower Canon is 31 to 64 (Xian to Wei Ji).
