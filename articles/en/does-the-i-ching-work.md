# Does the I Ching actually work? Prediction versus reflection

> This is a mirror. The **canonical, most up-to-date version** is at https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/does-the-i-ching-work.html

Written by [Wei Chun Chuang](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/about.html)・2026-11-24

*Whether the I Ching works depends on what you ask of it. Why it cannot predict events, the situations where it genuinely helps, how the Barnum effect explains readings that feel accurate, and four tests for telling a useful reading from a misleading one.*

---

“Does the I Ching actually work?” is the question beginners ask most often, and the hardest to answer in one line. The difficulty is not in the I Ching but in the word *work*. Are you asking whether it can tell you what will happen next week, or whether it can help you think something through? Those are two different questions with two different answers.

## Split the question in two

The first question: **can the I Ching predict events?** On all the testable evidence available, no. No controlled study shows that a hexagram cast with three coins predicts specific events better than chance. This site does not dodge that, and does not blur it with talk of sincerity.

The second question: **can it help a person think more clearly?** Many people find in practice that it can, and the mechanism can be explained without appealing to anything mysterious. The two are treated separately below.

## Why it is not an event predictor

Six throws of three coins produce one of 4,096 equally likely outcomes. The hexagram carries no information about the outside world; it is a set of symbols that a three-thousand-year commentary tradition has given meaning to. The meaning comes from the tradition, not from the physics of the throw.

The line texts are also compressed and open to several readings. “A hidden dragon; do not act” can be read as “the timing is wrong”, as “build your strength first”, or as “stay out of this”. Any statement open enough will find a matching fact after the event. Psychologists call this the Barnum effect, and it is not unique to the I Ching: astrology, tarot and personality quizzes all share it.

So when someone uses a hexagram to tell you that help will arrive within three months, or that an investment is certain to pay off, they have stopped reading the I Ching and started making claims with nothing behind them.

## Where it genuinely helps

What the I Ching really offers is **a structured frame for reflection**. Three things about it can be stated plainly:

- **It forces you to state the question.** Before casting you have to write the question down, and “should I take this job” turns into “if I take this job, what am I most worried about”. That step alone settles half the problem for many people.

- **It hands you an angle you would not have chosen.** Worry tends to run in the same loop. A line text gives you an outside starting point and makes you look again from somewhere else, much as talking to a friend or writing the problem out does.

- **It delays the decision a little.** Casting, looking up the hexagram and reading the lines takes a quarter of an hour. When feelings are running high, that gap has value in itself.

In other words, what helps is not what the hexagram “got right”, but how much you sorted out your own question while reading it.

## Four tests for a reading

The same hexagram can be read in a way that leaves you clearer, or in a way that leaves you dependent. Four tests:

- **Does it speak in certainties?** Words like *will*, *certainly* and *inevitably* leave the register of the original text, which is full of conditional terms about tendencies and prerequisites, not outcomes.

- **Does it show the original text?** When the reading and the source sit side by side, you can judge for yourself where the plain-language part came from. A conclusion with no source cannot be checked.

- **Does it show the derivation?** Going from six numbers to the primary hexagram, the changing lines and the resulting hexagram follows stated rules. A reading that skips this is asking you to trust a black box.

- **Does it leave the decision with you?** A good reading ends with what you now have to weigh, not with what you should do.

## What not to ask it

Three kinds of question should never be acted on from a hexagram: **medical** (symptoms, diagnosis, whether to see a doctor), **legal** (contracts, disputes, rights) and **financial** (what to buy, when to trade, whether to lend or borrow). Professionals exist for all three, and the cost of being wrong is real and often irreversible.

Explaining a physical symptom through a hexagram and putting off care is the most common and most dangerous version of this.

## A more honest way to use it

Treat the I Ching as a book of questions rather than a book of answers. When something is stuck, write the question down, cast, and as you read the line ask yourself: if this were true, what should I be watching now? Write your answer beside it. Come back a month later and you will find the useful part is what you wrote, not what the text said.

Used this way it will neither disappoint you nor leave your life to a one-in-4,096 chance.

## Common questions

**Is the I Ching accurate?**

If accuracy means predicting specific events, there is no testable evidence for it, and this site does not claim it. If it means helping you think a problem through and see an angle you had missed, many people find it useful in practice, and the mechanism is explainable: it forces you to state the question, supplies an outside viewpoint, and delays the decision a little.

**Why do the line texts so often feel apt?**

The texts are compressed and open to several readings, and any sufficiently open statement will find a matching fact after the event. Psychologists call this the Barnum effect, and astrology, tarot and personality quizzes all show it. That does not make reading worthless, but it does mean “it felt accurate” is not evidence.

**What should I not ask the I Ching?**

Medical, legal and financial questions. Symptoms and diagnosis belong with a doctor, contracts and rights with a lawyer, investing and borrowing with a qualified financial professional. Explaining a symptom through a hexagram and delaying care is the most dangerous misuse.

**How can I tell a misleading reading?**

Four tests: whether it speaks in certainties, whether it shows the original text so you can check it, whether it shows the derivation from six numbers to the primary and resulting hexagrams, and whether it ends by leaving the decision with you rather than making it for you.
