# How do you ask the I Ching a question?

---

Writing the question clearly matters more than rushing to cast. A good question is **specific, open-ended, about one thing, within your own power to act on, and honest**. When the question is clear, a single hexagram gives you a lot to work with; when it is vague, even an apt hexagram only buys you a vague reading. This article sets out five principles and a short checklist to run before you cast.

## Why does the wording change the reading?

The hexagram and line texts describe how a situation moves, not whether something will or will not happen. Whatever question you bring, you read the same words differently. A question is like a lamp: you see whatever it points at. So spending a minute to write the question down and sharpen it is usually worth more than casting again and again.

## Principle 1: turn a yes-or-no into an open question

"Should I take this job?" is a yes-or-no question, and it makes you watch only for a verdict while ignoring the process the line texts describe. Open it up: "What will I face if I take this job?" From the same hexagram, the open form yields the situation, the things to watch, and where it may be heading. Swap "should I", "will it", "is it ok" for "what will I face", "what should I watch for", "what would be a fitting way to do this".

## Principle 2: ask about one thing at a time

One question per casting is the traditional practice, and there is a practical reason for it: if you pack "change jobs, move house, and what about my relationship" into one throw, you cannot tell which one the hexagram is answering. Split a big question into a few smaller ones and cast once on each so every result reads cleanly.

## Principle 3: ask about what you can act on

The most useful question is one whose answer connects to your next move. Ask "Will he reply to my message?" and whatever the hexagram says, you can only wait. Ask instead "Is now a good time for me to reach out, and what should I keep in mind?" and the reading connects to something you can actually do. The I Ching is good at lighting up your own side of a situation and your sense of proportion, not at forecasting another person's behaviour.

## Principle 4: use a concrete time and setting

"How is my career?" is so broad that any hexagram can be forced to fit. Add a time and a setting and it comes into focus: "This quarter I am putting my energy into this project — is that a fitting focus?" A concrete question lets you check it later: looking back after a while, you can tell whether the text really echoed the situation or you talked yourself into a fit after the fact.

## Principle 5: be honest about what you really want to ask

Sometimes the question on the paper is not the one that is actually on your mind. "Will this investment pay off?" may really be "Am I avoiding sitting down and planning my money properly?" Before you cast, ask yourself once more, "What do I actually want to know?" — it often rewrites the question into something closer to the core and more honest. There is no mysticism here; it is simply thinking clearly.

## A checklist before you cast

- Write the question as a single sentence and read it back.

- Is it open-ended, or can it only be answered yes or no? Rewrite yes-or-no questions.

- Does it ask about just one thing? Split it if it bundles several.

- Does the answer connect to a next step I can take?

- Does it have a concrete time or setting?

- Is this really what I want to know?

Once all six pass, [cast with three coins](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/coin-casting.html) and read [the primary hexagram, the changing lines and the resulting hexagram](https://mm7382.github.io/learn/en/changing-lines.html).

## An honest note

Writing the question clearly is itself the most valuable part of the practice: it forces a vague worry into one concrete, actionable question. The I Ching does not predict outcomes and guarantees nothing. For medical, legal, financial or other major decisions, consult a qualified professional.

## FAQ

**Can I ask the I Ching a yes-or-no question?**

You can cast on one, but a yes-or-no frame makes you watch only for a verdict and miss the process the hexagram and line texts actually describe. Change "Should I take the job?" into "What will I face if I take the job?" and the same hexagram gives you far more to work with.

**Can I ask several questions at once?**

The usual practice is one question per casting. If you bundle several things together you cannot tell which one the hexagram is answering. Split a big question into a few smaller ones and cast once on each.

**Can I ask about someone else?**

The most useful questions are ones you can act on. "Will he reply to my message?" leaves you nothing to do; "Is now a good time for me to reach out, and what should I watch for?" gives a reading that connects to your next step.
